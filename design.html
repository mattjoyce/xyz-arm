What it is

A vertical 2020 spine carries a horizontal 2020 gantry on a four-wheel plate. A braided line over a pulley at the top of the spine balances the whole moving assembly, so the gantry is near-weightless and stays where you put it — the Dyson CSYS behaviour. The phone lies flat along the gantry, screen up, with only the camera module looking down. A thumbscrew locks the plate before each capture.

The design target is not rigidity. Because the mat carries ArUco fiducials and deskcam scale reads pixels-per-millimetre out of the picture itself, static deflection is absorbed by the pose solve. What is not absorbed is movement during the exposure or drift between calibration and shot. So the rig is designed for stability, not stiffness — which is why the lock matters more than the wall thickness.

Decisions

Settled

Phone runs on battery, no tether to the frame

A USB-C cable dragging on a moving gantry applies 0.5–2 N that varies with height, so a counterweight could only ever be correct at one position. Cutting it makes a single counterweight correct across the whole travel. Battery-only also runs cooler than charging, which matters — the noise floor rises with sensor temperature.

Settled

Phone lies flat along the gantry, screen up

Camera faces the mat, screen stays readable and tappable from above. The camera bump becomes the lowest point, so the extrusion never clips the cone. Cradle is an open U gripping the two long edges only — nothing under the camera to crush it.

Settled

Clamp the phone back to the extrusion through a thermal pad

500 mm of 6063 is roughly 230 g at 900 J/kg·K — about 207 J/K of thermal mass sitting exactly where the heat is. A £2 pad turns the gantry into a heatsink and directly attacks the HOT severe throttling that degrades long sessions.

Settled

Camera sits flat — no tilt, no roll

Not a compromise; the right call for an instrument. An articulated head has an alignment error that changes every time it is touched. A fixed head has a constant one, measured once and rectified by the fiducials from then on. It also keeps the rig a pure XYZ translation stage, which is the premise. Put the one-time adjustment at the spine's base instead — three levelling screws or shims, set plumb once, never touched in use.

Settled

Hand-positioned, with a lock

Counterweight for easy positioning, thumbscrew for the shot. The lock deletes the vibration problem rather than managing it. Because the axis is balanced the lock holds almost nothing — which is good, because it must not change the geometry when it bites.

Settled

Light magnets to a steel strip under the gantry

A 16 × 1.5 mm mild-steel strip taped or screwed along the gantry's underside. The Olight's tailcap grabs anywhere on it, so the light slides to any offset by hand — no arm, no bracket, and the full 500 mm of reach is available for a genuinely shallow grazing angle.

At 1.02 kg hanging, the braid sees about 10.0 N — roughly 7 % of a realistic break load for 0.37 mm eight-strand, nearer 15 kg than the 50 lb on the label. At that fraction strength is not the question; creep is.

Creep costs travel, not balance. Tension is set by the hanging mass, and that does not change as the line lengthens — so the gantry stays balanced however much it stretches. What moves is where the ends of travel fall: the counterweight bottoms out sooner and the top of the stroke goes first. The adjuster restores it in seconds. Budget a tweak every few months, not a rebuild.

Run it 5 % light, on purpose

A perfectly balanced axis creeps and, once motorised, leaves the leadscrew nut floating between flanks — the worst case for backlash. A slight downward bias settles the manual rig predictably and keeps the nut loaded on one flank later. Make the weight a stack of washers or a tube you add shot to.

Constraint found

Superseded by the mounting decision. While the rig stood on its own base the weight had to fit alongside the spine, which forced a long slim slab and ruled out steel shot on density. Hanging it below the desk removes the envelope entirely: any shape, and steel shot is back.

The governing constraint

Not the line, and not the wheels' load rating. It is the tipping moment where the gantry meets the spine. With the carriage at 400 mm the contributions total about 2.4 N·m with the light out at the far end, and the plate can only resist that as a force couple between its two wheel pairs. Vertical wheel spacing d sets the load — and eats travel.

150 mm is the pick. It keeps wheel load comfortable for polycarbonate wheels and still leaves 310 mm of travel — which, as the working band below shows, is more than the optics need.

Watch this

With no tilt or roll adjustment in the head, the gantry-to-plate joint has to be square by construction — there is nothing to dial out afterwards. Use two bolts spaced along the extrusion, never two at one station in a single slot, which leaves the joint free to roll about the bolt axis and tips the camera sideways.

Why the light goes on the carriage

The phone's own torch is coaxial with the lens — it shows colour but not shape. An offset light gives oblique illumination that reveals topography, which is what you want on solder joints and silkscreen. The steel strip makes that offset a free choice along the whole gantry, and the reach is the point: a short arm on the carriage tops out near 100 mm, a moderate angle at best. The strip reaches 400 mm.

So the strip buys reach, and the discipline is to slide the light along with the carriage rather than leave it parked. Mark the strip once and the angle becomes repeatable by eye.

Two gotchas

Buy carbon steel, not stainless. 304 and 316 are austenitic and effectively non-magnetic, so a stainless rule may not hold the light at all. Take a magnet to the shop. And keep the magnet off the camera end. The 6a’s main camera is confirmed OIS, and both OIS and the focus actuator are voice coils working against magnets, so a neodymium tailcap parked beside the module can make focus hunt. Past about 100 mm it is a non-issue — and every useful raking angle above already puts it further than that.

The working band

The Pixel 6a main camera is a 1/2.55″ sensor, 5.645 × 4.234 mm at 1.4 µm, behind a 4.40 mm lens — so 65.4° horizontal, 51.4° vertical, and a frame width of 1.283 × height. Derived from the sensor rather than estimated, and they confirm the 500 mm spine.

Deflection, and why it doesn't matter much

The section is no longer assumed. Integrating OpenBuilds' own DXF gives 171.4 mm² of aluminium and Ix = Iy = 0.699 cm⁴, which checks out at 463 g/m — the parse returns a 20.00 × 20.00 bounding box and a centroid on the origin, so it read the profile correctly. With 6063-T5 at E = 69 GPa that is EI = 4.82 × 10⁸ N·mm².

The gantry then droops 0.21 mm at the camera with the carriage at 400 mm and the light at a middling offset, 0.26 mm with the light parked at the far tip. Tilt runs 0.04–0.06°. Working and drawings are in datasheets/.

That droop is a change in standoff, so what it costs is scale: about 0.15 % at a 150 mm working distance. Two things absorb it. Depth of field at 98 mm is roughly ±1.3 mm, so a quarter-millimetre never defocuses. And scale is measured from the fiducials in every frame rather than assumed from the mechanism, so a repeatable 0.15 % shift is read out, not designed out.

The conclusion is the one that shapes the build: chase settling time and drift, not section modulus. Which is an argument for Delrin wheels over the clear polycarbonate ones — they damp better and are kinder to the bearing bore. The clear wheels will work; just don't over-preload the eccentrics, and expect to wait longer after each nudge.

Motor-ready, at no cost today

The later version with tiny motors and spindles needs two provisions that are free to add now, and one that is already in the design.

  • A boss on the plate with a clearance hole and two tapped M3s for an anti-backlash leadscrew nut. Unused on the manual build.
  • A motor mount face at the top of the spine, left blank.
  • The counterweight itself. A NEMA8 has almost no torque; a balanced axis is the only way a small motor moves this gantry at all. It is not a manual-build feature to be removed later — it is what makes the motorised version possible.

Open

Button window

Power and volume sit on one long edge — exactly the edge a U-cradle grips. With no relief window the cradle holds them down and the phone sleeps, screenshots and rides the volume. Measure and cut, rather than design around.

Settled

Braided line, not a belt

Chosen for bulk and part count rather than mass — the two differ by about two grams in a 1078 g budget, so weight was never the argument. What 0.37 mm buys is a pulley an order of magnitude smaller than a 6 mm belt needs, routing that fits anywhere, no printed belt clip, and silence. The accepted trade is creep, and it costs less than it sounds.

Settled

Capstan tie-off, and a keeper on the pulley

Braid is slippery and knots in it lose 20–30 %. Three or four turns around a printed post before a half hitch sheds nearly all the load to friction, so the knot carries almost nothing — and no clamp part, which suits the minimal build. Separately: a balanced line goes slack if the gantry is lifted faster than the weight follows, and slack cord jumps the groove. One pin or a printed shroud across the pulley face prevents that.

Resolved

Counterweight hangs below the desk

The line drops through a hole in the desktop to a weight in the knee space, so shape stops mattering: a 60 × 80 mm pot of steel shot makes the 1.02 kg, no lead needed. A removable lid turns the fill into the bias adjuster. Size the hole for clearance (~10 mm), not fit — the line pays 310 mm through it every stroke, and a braid sawing on bare timber is a real wear point, whereas a truly vertical run through an oversized hole never touches anything. A guide tube under the desk stops it swinging, stops it being kicked, and catches it if the line ever parts.

Settled

Clamped or screwed to the desk

Deletes the ballast problem outright: 2.18 N·m over 50 mm of clamp jaw is a 44 N couple, against the 3.55 kg of ballast a 140 mm freestanding base would have needed. It also drops the mount to 5–10 mm tall instead of 30–50, handing back travel at the bottom of the working band where the 98 mm focus limit bites. The catch: the bracket is now the softest thing in the chain, and PETG is about 35× floppier than 6063 for the same section — so keep metal in the moment path, and load any printed part in compression or shear rather than bending.
